# Adaptive Reuse Variants Research

Two real-world buildings, six architecturally plausible variants each grounded in documented precedents.

---

## Building 1: Calgary Board of Education Building (515 Macleod Trail SE)

### The Existing Building

The CBE headquarters is a five-storey Brutalist office building completed in 1969 as part of an urban-renewal project, at a cost of $3.5 million. Its exterior is defined by hard-edged, board-formed exposed concrete with a repetitive grid of deeply recessed windows. The facade reads as a monolithic concrete mass punctuated by shadow-box window bays. Outside the main entrance stand the "Brotherhood of Mankind" sculptures -- sleek, 21-foot-tall bronze figures that serve as the CBE's emblem.

The building has been vacant since the CBE vacated in 2011. It was sold in 2012 for $36.5 million but remains unused as of 2025. Calgary's Downtown Development Incentive Program offers $75/sqft for office-to-residential conversions, and over 21 conversion projects totaling 2.7 million square feet have been conditionally approved across the downtown core. This building, however, has not yet received conversion funding.

**Key physical characteristics for variant design:**
- Five storeys, concrete frame construction
- Board-formed exposed concrete exterior walls
- Deeply recessed, grid-pattern window openings
- Flat roof
- Ground-level plaza with the Brotherhood of Mankind sculptures
- Located on Macleod Trail, a major north-south arterial in downtown Calgary
- Approximately 100,000+ sqft of floor area

---

### Variant 1: "The Living Concrete" -- Biophilic Deep-Green Adaptive Reuse

**Concept:** Transform the Brutalist concrete shell into an eco-brutalist living building that embraces its raw concrete bones while wrapping them in vegetation, light, and sustainable systems. The building becomes a demonstration project for Calgary's climate adaptation.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **The Barbican Centre, London** -- Brutalist complex with a conservatory containing 1,500+ plant species, now operating on 100% renewable energy
- **Bosco Verticale, Milan** -- Bold geometric forms integrated with cascading vegetation on concrete balconies
- **Hotel Marcel, New Haven** -- Marcel Breuer's 1969 Brutalist Pirelli Building converted to the USA's first Passive House-certified, net-zero energy hotel
- **Swedish "Million Program" retrofits** -- 1960s-70s concrete buildings deep-retrofitted to Passive House standard at ~EUR200/sqft, achieving net-positive energy production

**Specific exterior changes:**

1. **Facade green wall system:** The deeply recessed concrete window bays become planting troughs. Each recessed bay receives a stainless-steel planter frame bolted to the existing concrete reveals. Hardy, drought-tolerant species suited to Calgary's Zone 3 climate (sedum, sempervivum, native grasses, creeping juniper) cascade from each bay. The concrete grid remains fully visible -- the plants occupy the voids between the structural ribs, creating a checkerboard of raw concrete and living green. In winter, the evergreen species (juniper, mugo pine) remain while deciduous plants go dormant, giving a seasonally shifting appearance.

2. **Rooftop greenhouse/solarium addition:** A lightweight steel-and-glass greenhouse structure sits atop the flat roof, visible from the street as a glowing crystalline volume contrasting with the heavy concrete base. The greenhouse is set back from the roof edge by 3 meters so the original concrete parapet remains the dominant roofline. Interior program: year-round urban agriculture (microgreens, herbs, lettuce), community growing space, and a public cafe. The greenhouse uses triple-glazed, argon-filled panels with automated louvres for summer ventilation.

3. **South facade solar integration:** The south-facing elevation receives building-integrated photovoltaic (BIPV) panels mounted as shading fins within the existing window recesses. These angled metal-and-glass fins project ~600mm from the facade, casting diagonal shadow lines across the concrete while generating electricity. Their angle is optimized for Calgary's 51-degree latitude. Color: dark charcoal with a subtle blue photovoltaic shimmer.

4. **Ground-level transformation:** The existing plaza is regraded into a sunken rain garden and bioswale system. Native prairie grasses (blue grama, fescue, prairie dropseed) replace hardscape. A new canopy of weathering steel and translucent polycarbonate panels extends from the building entrance over the Brotherhood of Mankind sculptures, framing them within a garden setting rather than a concrete plaza. Permeable pavers replace asphalt.

5. **Window upgrades:** All existing single-pane windows are replaced with triple-glazed, thermally broken units. The new frames are powder-coated dark bronze to read as shadow lines within the deep concrete recesses, maintaining the original shadow-box effect while dramatically improving thermal performance.

6. **Exterior insulation (selective):** Where the concrete is in poor condition, an exterior mineral wool insulation system is applied and finished with a lime-based render that mimics the board-formed texture. Where concrete is in good condition, it is left exposed and treated with a breathable silane sealer. This creates a patchwork of restored original concrete and sympathetically textured new render -- both in the same grey tone.

**Interior program:** Mixed-use -- ground floor public market/food hall, floors 2-3 co-working and maker spaces, floors 4-5 affordable artist live/work studios, rooftop greenhouse cafe and urban farm.

On the flat roof, a glowing steel-and-glass greenhouse structure is set back from the parapet edge. The south facade has angled dark charcoal photovoltaic shading fins projecting from window recesses. At ground level, a rain garden with native prairie grasses surrounds tall bronze figurative sculptures beneath a weathering steel canopy. Triple-glazed windows with dark bronze frames sit deep in the concrete bays. Winter scene with some snow on the ground, evergreen plants remaining green against grey concrete, greenhouse glowing warmly from within. ### Variant 2: "The Concrete Canvas" -- Artist Lofts and Cultural Hub

**Concept:** Convert the building into a mixed-use cultural anchor combining affordable artist live/work lofts with ground-floor gallery and performance space. The Brutalist exterior is celebrated rather than hidden, with strategic insertions of color, transparency, and signage that announce the building's creative identity.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **SESC Pompeia, Sao Paulo** -- Lina Bo Bardi's conversion of an industrial complex into a cultural center, juxtaposing exposed brick/concrete with bold color insertions and aerial walkways
- **The Standard London** -- 1970s Brutalist office building converted to boutique hotel with three new stories added on top, bold design interventions against the preserved concrete shell
- **Berlin 1970s block conversions** -- Brutalist residential blocks repurposed as artist lofts, cutting new-build emissions by 70%
- **Calgary's own conversion program** -- 21 approved projects demonstrating the city's regulatory pathway and incentive structure for office-to-residential conversion

**Specific exterior changes:**

1. **New penthouse addition:** A two-storey lightweight addition sits on the existing roof, constructed in cross-laminated timber (CLT) with a standing-seam zinc cladding. The addition is intentionally different in material and proportion from the concrete base -- its walls are clad in vertical zinc panels with a warm grey patina, and its windows are large, horizontal ribbon windows. This addition houses the top-floor artist studios with north-facing skylights. The contrast between heavy concrete below and light timber/zinc above is deliberate and legible.

2. **Ground-floor transparency:** The solid concrete panels at ground level are partially removed and replaced with full-height steel-framed glazing, creating a visible gallery storefront. The concrete structural columns remain exposed inside the glass, maintaining the rhythm of the original facade while opening the ground floor to the street. The gallery space is visible from Macleod Trail, with rotating art installations visible through the glass at night.

3. **Color accent insertion:** One vertical bay of the facade (a single column-width strip from ground to roofline) is infilled with a deep vermillion-red perforated metal screen instead of concrete panels. This "scar" of color cuts through the grey facade as a vertical beacon, signaling the building's cultural identity. At night, interior lighting glows through the perforations. The red references the Brotherhood of Mankind sculptures' bronze warmth.

4. **Exterior staircase/fire escape as sculptural element:** A new external steel staircase is attached to the east facade as both a code-required second egress and an architectural feature. Constructed from weathering steel (cor-ten), it zigzags across the facade in an expressive pattern, its warm rust color contrasting with the cool grey concrete. Landings at each floor double as small outdoor terraces for the live/work units.

5. **Signage and wayfinding:** Large-scale stainless-steel dimensional lettering is mounted directly to the concrete facade, spelling the building's new name (e.g., "CBE ARTS" or "515 CULTURAL"). The letters are 900mm tall, sans-serif, mounted on standoffs that cast shadows on the concrete behind. Subtle uplighting at night.

6. **Window treatment variety:** Rather than uniform window replacement, each artist loft tenant can choose from three approved window configurations: (a) full-height clear glass, (b) upper-transom operable with lower fixed, or (c) opaque insulated panel with a smaller vision window. This creates an organic, varied facade that reflects the individual character of each studio -- a "quilt" effect across the grid.

**Interior program:** Ground floor public gallery and black-box performance space, floors 2-4 artist live/work lofts (1BR and 2BR units with double-height studio spaces), floor 5 shared workshops (printmaking, ceramics, woodworking), penthouse addition houses large-format painting studios and a residents' lounge.

The heavy board-formed concrete grid facade is preserved, but the ground floor has been opened up with full-height steel-framed glass revealing a gallery space within. A two-storey penthouse addition in vertical zinc cladding with ribbon windows sits on the roof, lighter in tone and material than the concrete base. A zigzagging weathering steel (cor-ten) external staircase with rust-orange patina is attached to the east facade. Windows across the upper floors show varied configurations -- some fully glazed, some with opaque panels -- creating an organic patchwork. Large stainless-steel dimensional letters spell "515 CULTURAL" on the concrete facade. The Brotherhood of Mankind bronze sculptures stand at the entrance. Evening scene, warm interior light spilling through windows and the red perforated screen. Photorealistic architectural rendering, Calgary downtown context.

### Variant 3: "Heritage Modernized" -- Preserved and Restored with Contemporary Addition

**Concept:** A respectful heritage restoration of the Brutalist exterior combined with a bold contemporary glass-and-steel addition that extends the building without competing with it. The approach follows the principle of "honest contrast" -- new work is clearly distinguishable from original fabric.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **Neue Nationalgalerie, Berlin** -- Mies van der Rohe's glass pavilion museum, restored by David Chipperfield Architects (2021) with meticulous attention to original materials while upgrading all building systems
- **The Acre, London** -- Gensler retained 80% of the original Brutalist structure while reimagining it as a community-focused workplace
- **Hotel Marcel, New Haven** -- Breuer's Pirelli Building restored and converted while maintaining the original architectural character, achieving net-zero energy

**Specific exterior changes:**

1. **Concrete restoration:** The entire concrete facade is cleaned using a controlled micro-abrasive technique (not pressure washing, which damages board-form texture). Cracks are repaired with color-matched cementitious fill. Spalled areas are patched using formwork that replicates the original board-form pattern. A breathable silane water repellent is applied. The goal is a facade that looks exactly as it did in 1969, but structurally sound and protected.

2. **Window restoration to original specification:** Windows are replaced with thermally upgraded units that replicate the original aluminum frame profiles and sightlines. Slim double-glazed units with warm-edge spacers fit within the original openings. Frame color matches the original anodized aluminum tone. From the street, the fenestration pattern is indistinguishable from the 1969 original.

3. **Contemporary glass link addition:** A new three-storey glass-and-steel volume is constructed adjacent to the east elevation, connected to the original building by enclosed glass bridges at each floor. The addition is a simple rectangular prism with a unitized curtain wall of clear low-iron glass and slender black steel mullions. Its footprint is smaller than the original building's. The glass volume is deliberately transparent and lightweight, making the heavy concrete of the original appear even more monumental by contrast. The addition houses a new lobby, vertical circulation (elevators, accessible stairs), and additional commercial space.

4. **Rooftop mechanical screening:** The existing rooftop mechanical equipment is screened behind a new perforated aluminum enclosure, powder-coated in a dark charcoal that recedes against the sky. The screen is set back from the roof edge so as not to alter the parapet silhouette.

5. **Ground-level heritage landscape:** The plaza is restored with period-appropriate materials -- exposed aggregate concrete pavers, integrated concrete benches in the same mix as the building. The Brotherhood of Mankind sculptures are cleaned and re-mounted on restored plinths. New LED ground-wash lighting illuminates both the sculptures and the base of the building facade at night, emphasizing the board-form texture. A single mature tree (Green Ash or similar hardy Calgary species) is planted in a new concrete planter to soften the plaza without cluttering it.

6. **Heritage plaque and interpretation:** A bronze heritage plaque is mounted near the entrance, and subtle interpretive signage explains the building's significance within Calgary's Brutalist heritage. Small QR code markers link to a digital walking tour.

**Interior program:** Converted to Class A office space with modern amenities -- ground floor lobby and cafe, floors 2-5 open-plan and partitioned office suites, contemporary glass addition houses a new atrium lobby with exposed concrete views into the original structure.

**Image generation prompt:**
> Five-storey Brutalist concrete office building, meticulously restored to its 1969 original appearance. Clean, sharp board-formed exposed concrete with a precise grid of deeply recessed windows, aluminum window frames in original anodized finish. Adjacent to the east facade, a new three-storey glass-and-steel rectangular volume with slender black mullions and clear glass, connected to the original building by enclosed glass bridges at each floor. The glass addition is deliberately transparent and lighter, making the concrete original appear even more monumental. At ground level, an exposed-aggregate concrete plaza with integrated concrete benches surrounds the restored Brotherhood of Mankind bronze sculptures, illuminated by LED ground-wash lighting. A single mature tree softens the plaza. The rooftop mechanical equipment is screened by a dark charcoal perforated aluminum enclosure set back from the parapet. Daytime scene, clear blue Calgary sky, sharp winter sunlight casting deep shadows in the window recesses. Photorealistic architectural rendering, heritage restoration style.

## Building 2: Farnsworth House (Plano, Illinois)

### The Existing Building

Designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and completed in 1951, the Farnsworth House is a single-room weekend retreat for Dr. Edith Farnsworth, located on a 60-acre estate along the Fox River, 55 miles southwest of Chicago. It is a National Historic Landmark (2006) and one of the most celebrated residential buildings of the twentieth century.

**Key physical characteristics for variant design:**
- Three horizontal planes: elevated terrace platform, floor slab, and roof slab, all in travertine/concrete
- Eight wide-flange I-beam steel columns, painted white, welded to the floor and roof structures
- Floor-to-ceiling single-pane glass walls on all four sides, set between the columns via steel mullions
- Elevated approximately 5 feet above grade on the columns (flood plain design)
- Rectangular plan, approximately 77 ft x 29 ft (23.5m x 8.8m)
- A central utility core in primavera wood containing kitchen, bathrooms, and fireplace
- A lower entry terrace/porch, connected to the main floor by steps
- Surrounded by mature deciduous trees (sugar maples, elms, oaks) and a meadow sloping to the Fox River
- All structural connections are plug-welded and polished smooth -- the hallmark Miesian "seamless" aesthetic
- Painted an elegant white (not the industrial black Mies used on larger projects)

---

### Variant 1: "Four Seasons Pavilion" -- Seasonal/Weather-Responsive Adaptation

**Concept:** The same house, same site, but with the addition of a wood-louver exterior screen system that responds to seasonal conditions. This is not a renovation of the heritage building but a respectful "what if" -- imagining Mies had designed a climate-responsive layer. The glass transparency is preserved but modulated.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **Philip Johnson's Glass House** -- Johnson later added the Painting Gallery (1965) and Sculpture Gallery (1970) with different enclosure strategies to the same property, demonstrating how a glass house concept evolves
- **Glenn Murcutt's houses (Australia)** -- Mies-influenced steel-and-glass pavilions with operable timber louver screens for sun control and ventilation, widely cited as the natural evolution of Miesian transparency in warm climates
- **Olson Kundig's Studhorse (Methow Valley, WA)** -- Multiple pavilion structures using operable steel shutters and cor-ten steel panels designed for four-season use

**Specific exterior description:**

1. **Exterior wood louver screen:** A freestanding timber screen system surrounds the house at a distance of 1.2 meters from the glass walls. The screen is composed of vertical white-oak louvers, 50mm wide, spaced at 75mm intervals, mounted in a slender white-painted steel frame that echoes the proportions of the house's I-beam columns. The louver frame rests on its own minimal steel footings, independent of the house structure. The louvers are fixed (not operable) but their spacing allows dappled light and views through while providing partial sun shading and a sense of enclosure.

2. **Seasonal appearance -- Summer:** The white oak louvers are a warm honey-gold, the surrounding trees are in full leaf, and the louver screen creates a layered veil of shadow across the glass. The house is partially screened but its white steel frame remains visible through the louver gaps. The effect is of a pavilion within a pavilion -- a cage of light and shadow.

3. **Seasonal appearance -- Autumn:** The oak louvers have weathered to a silver-grey tone over the years. The surrounding sugar maples and oaks blaze in red, orange, and gold. Fallen leaves collect on the travertine terrace. The louver screen reads as a neutral grey frame that amplifies the warm foliage colors visible through and beyond it. The glass reflects the autumn canopy.

4. **Seasonal appearance -- Winter:** Snow collects on the horizontal steel frame members and on the louver tops, creating thin white lines against the grey wood. The bare tree branches are visible through the louver gaps. The house interior is warmly lit, glowing through the louver screen as a lantern effect -- soft stripes of warm light alternating with dark louver shadows. Snow blankets the meadow and the lower terrace.

5. **Seasonal appearance -- Spring:** New green shoots and wildflowers carpet the meadow. The louvers have a fresh grey-silver patina. Morning mist rises from the Fox River, partially obscuring the house so that only the horizontal planes (white roof and floor slab) and the vertical louver lines are legible through the haze. Budding trees create a soft green halo above.

### Variant 2: "The River Gallery" -- Scaled-Up Pavilion as Art Gallery/Retreat

**Concept:** The Farnsworth House design language -- white steel frame, floor-to-ceiling glass, elevated on columns, travertine floors -- expanded to approximately 2.5x the footprint to serve as a small contemporary art gallery and boutique architectural retreat. Same proportions, same materials, same relationship to landscape, but a larger program.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **Neue Nationalgalerie, Berlin (1968)** -- Mies's own scaled-up glass pavilion museum, with a 65m-square floating black steel roof on cruciform columns, glass curtain walls, and a sunken gallery below the main platform. This is literally "the Farnsworth House scaled up as a museum" by the same architect.
- **Museum of Fine Arts Houston, Cullinan Hall** -- Mies's 10,000-sqft fan-shaped exhibition gallery with glass curtain walls
- **Barcelona Pavilion (reconstructed 1986)** -- Mies's glass/steel/stone pavilion at monumental public scale

**Specific exterior description:**

1. **Overall form:** A rectangular plan of approximately 60m x 18m (roughly 2.5x the Farnsworth footprint), elevated 1.5 meters above grade on white-painted wide-flange steel columns. The proportions maintain the Farnsworth's approximate 2.6:1 length-to-width ratio. Two horizontal planes -- floor slab and roof slab -- with continuous floor-to-ceiling glass walls between.

2. **Structural system:** Sixteen white-painted steel I-beam columns (doubled from the Farnsworth's eight) in the same wide-flange profile, welded to the floor and roof edge beams with the same plug-welded, polished-smooth connections. The columns are positioned at the facade line, attached to the outside of the floor and roof slabs as in the original. The roof overhangs the glass walls by 1 meter on all sides, creating a sheltering shadow line.

3. **Glass walls:** Floor-to-ceiling clear low-iron glass panels set between slender white-painted steel mullions, replicating the Farnsworth's minimal glazing system but with modern high-performance insulated glass units. On the long south elevation, every third glass bay is an operable sliding panel for ventilation during exhibition openings.

4. **Entry sequence:** Like the Farnsworth, a lower terrace/platform at one end serves as the entry, with broad travertine steps rising to the main gallery floor. The entry terrace is generous enough for outdoor sculpture display. A slender steel canopy extends from the main roof to shelter the entry steps.

5. **Interior visible through glass:** A single open gallery hall with polished travertine floors. Two freestanding primavera-wood-clad utility cores (containing climate control, restrooms, storage, and a small cafe) are visible as warm wood volumes within the glass box -- a direct scaling of the Farnsworth's single core into a paired arrangement.

6. **Landscape setting:** Sited in a similar riparian meadow, the building is oriented with its long axis parallel to a river or water feature. Mature deciduous trees surround but do not crowd the structure. A gravel path approaches from the south.

### Variant 3: "Dark Mirror" -- Cor-Ten Steel and Blackened Steel Reinterpretation

**Concept:** The identical Farnsworth House proportions, plan, and structural system, but reinterpreted in dark materials: blackened/dark-patina steel structure instead of white-painted, cor-ten (weathering) steel panels replacing some glass bays, and tinted glass where glass remains. The same "almost nothing" minimalism, but with a brooding, earth-toned character that connects to the landscape differently.

**Real-world precedents:**
- **Philip Johnson's Glass House (1949)** -- The "dark twin" of the Farnsworth, with a black-painted steel frame and charcoal-grey brick base cylinder, demonstrating how the same glass pavilion language reads completely differently in dark materials
- **Desert Nomad House by Rick Joy (Tucson, AZ)** -- Cor-ten steel volumes elevated above the desert floor, with floor-to-ceiling glass openings between rusted steel walls
- **Miner Road House by Faulkner Architects (Oakland Hills, CA)** -- Cor-ten steel fins extending from a glass-and-steel house, providing sun shading and material warmth
- **Y House (Canada)** -- Cor-ten steel facade with floor-to-ceiling windows on a rural retreat near the Rocky Mountains

**Specific exterior description:**

1. **Steel frame:** The eight I-beam columns and all visible structural steel (floor edge beams, roof edge beams, mullions) are finished in a blackened hot-patina steel -- not painted, but chemically treated to a deep charcoal-black with a subtle metallic sheen. The welded connections are polished smooth in the Miesian manner but read as dark joints rather than white ones. The effect is the Farnsworth's skeleton rendered in shadow.

2. **Cor-ten steel infill panels:** On the long north elevation, three of the glass bays are replaced with cor-ten weathering steel panels that have developed a rich, variegated rust-orange patina. These solid panels provide thermal mass, privacy for the bedroom zone, and a material warmth that contrasts with the glass. The cor-ten panels are flush-mounted in the same plane as the glass, maintaining the flat facade. The transition from glass to steel is clean -- a thin black steel reveal separates the two materials.

3. **Tinted glass:** The remaining glass walls are low-iron glass with a very subtle grey tint (approximately 70% visible light transmission vs. the original's clear glass). The tint reduces interior visibility slightly from the outside during daylight, creating a more reflective, mirror-like quality. In the evening, when interiors are lit, the house glows warmly through the dark frame -- the tint disappears and the transparency returns.

4. **Floor and roof slabs:** The travertine floor is replaced with a honed basalt (dark grey, nearly black) slab. The roof slab is clad in a thin blackened steel fascia. The two horizontal planes read as dark bands rather than the original's warm-white limestone.

5. **Entry terrace:** The lower terrace platform is paved in the same dark basalt. The steps between terrace and main floor are blackened steel plate with anti-slip texture. The entire base reads as a dark plinth hovering above the meadow, rather than the original's light limestone shelf.

6. **Core material:** The interior utility core (visible through the glass) is clad in charred timber (shou sugi ban) instead of primavera wood -- a black-brown surface that continues the dark material palette while introducing organic texture.

7. **Landscape relationship:** Against the same meadow and deciduous trees, the dark house acts as a shadow or void in the landscape rather than a bright object. In autumn, the cor-ten rust panels harmonize with fall foliage. In winter, the dark frame stands in high contrast to snow. In summer, the dark steel absorbs into the tree shadows.
