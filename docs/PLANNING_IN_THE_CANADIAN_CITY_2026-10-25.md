# Planning in the Canadian city

## A teaching framework for the archetype catalogue

Canadian urban form is best explained through overlapping histories of land,
housing, work, transport, public space and governance. Architecture helps students
recognise those histories, but a facade cannot establish a development's tenure,
planning process or social outcomes. International examples make the comparisons
stronger when their own origins remain visible.

The classification therefore keeps five questions distinct:

| Question | Example |
| --- | --- |
| What is its use and building form? | Housing; fourplex |
| What aesthetic or architectural tradition does it reference? | Contemporary gabled |
| Which design era does it relate to? | Contemporary; broadly 2000–present |
| Which planning ideas can it help students investigate? | Missing-middle housing, compact infill, complete neighbourhoods |
| What Canadian and international connections explain it? | Current Canadian housing choice; older house-scale multi-unit precedents internationally |

The dated ranges describe **approximate design associations**. They are not the
construction dates of real buildings. A newly authored Craftsman cottage can
reference 1900–1930 architecture; a contemporary office can draw on postwar
Modernism. This approach also separates the history of a building form from a
recent name for it: Opticos introduced the term missing middle in 2010 for types
with many pre 1940 precedents. [Missing Middle Housing](https://missingmiddlehousing.com/).

This pass covers 196 exact current local choices: 97 buildings, 53 streets/pathways
and 46 parks/open spaces. Eight inherited reference conflicts remain held.
Seven independently reviewed original building candidates receive research
records without entering the student catalogue. A separate 521-family coverage
index reconciles 497 legacy families with 24 current-only park/street families,
and distinguishes reviewed variants from 343 source-only families. It does not
extend one reviewed variant's dates to every other variant in its family.

## Overlapping eras for introductory browsing

| Era | Main planning questions | Examples to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier traditions, before c. 1850 | Whose land, settlement systems and local knowledge shape the city? How do ports, colonial surveys and inherited forms organise access? | Georgian influences, Kyoto machiya, canal-city references; Indigenous planning as longstanding and continuing |
| Industrial and streetcar city, c. 1850–1914 | How do rail, industry, small plots, shops and housing relate? Who gains access and who is excluded? | Main streets, Montréal duplexes, railway stations, iron-and-glass markets, industrial buildings |
| Reform and interwar city, c. 1900–1945 | How do reformers connect housing, health, green space and civic identity? How do new design movements change buildings? | Garden suburbs, Craftsman/Prairie School houses, Art Deco, Bauhaus, De Stijl |
| Postwar expansion, c. 1945–1975 | How can cities accommodate rapid growth? What follows from road hierarchy, separation of uses and redevelopment? | Small-house programmes, Don Mills, retail strips, slab apartments, concrete parking structures, civic Modernism |
| Late 20th-century change, c. 1970–2000 | How do conservation, community action, regeneration, transit and new housing approaches change established districts? | Rehabilitation, adaptive reuse, mixed-income neighbourhoods, Postmodern civic buildings, Vancouver tower-and-podium forms |
| Contemporary city, c. 2000–present | How do housing choice, climate response, daily access and reconciliation influence development? | Fourplexes, courtyard housing, Complete Streets, green infrastructure, transit-linked intensification, Indigenous-led development |
| Era not specified | What additional evidence would distinguish the component's historical context? | Generic paths, courts, working enclosures and some long-running landscape traditions |

The categories overlap deliberately. A 1905 house belongs to industrial-city
growth and early 20th-century reform conversations. Indigenous planning is not
confined to the first row. The Canadian Institute of Planners recognises both
the profession's role in colonial land division and the suppression of
Indigenous planning systems, and the continuing responsibilities of planning
practice. [CIP reconciliation](https://www.cip-icu.ca/reconciliation/).

## Historical connections worth teaching

### Land and governance before style

Settlement, land allocation and infrastructure decisions shape what students
can build long before a roof or facade is chosen. Canadian city planning
connects municipal decisions, provincial/territorial frameworks, federal housing
and infrastructure activity, and Indigenous governance. These relationships vary
by place and cannot be inferred from the appearance of a model.

Sen̓áḵw offers a contemporary example grounded in the Squamish Nation's own
account: ancestral village lands were taken, part was returned through a 2003 court
decision, and the Nation voted in 2019 to pursue development. The case is about
land, authority, community priorities and urban development. It is not a generic
Indigenous architectural aesthetic. No catalogue item receives an Indigenous
planning label from timber, landscaping or motifs.
[Squamish Nation](https://www.squamish.net/partnerships-entities/partnerships/senakw/).

### The industrial city and inherited mixed-use neighbourhoods

Historic main streets, urban houses, markets and working buildings allow students
to examine short trips, small frontages, employment and changing uses. Toronto's
Cabbagetown history records late 19th-century houses, subsequent industrial and
commercial buildings, and later conversions and conservation interest. This is
a useful reminder that urban fabric changes through adaptation as well as
replacement. [City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/planning-development/planning-studies-initiatives/cabbagetown-southwest-heritage-conservation-district-plan/research-and-analysis-cabbagetown-southwest-hcd-plan/).

The Plateau duplex and contemporary fourplex can therefore share a housing-form
discussion while keeping distinct historical and aesthetic records. A railway
hotel, market and station also offer different lessons from a residential street.
The Parisian boulevard, Amsterdam canal and Kyoto machiya retain their own
contexts as international comparisons, not alternative labels for Canadian sites.

### Reform, Garden City and civic improvement

Garden City thinking linked settlement to landscape, work, housing and community
benefit. The movement's institutional history includes the 1899 Garden City
Association. In Canada, Thomas Adams worked on garden suburbs including
Hydrostone and Lindenlea, and helped establish the planning institute in 1919.
A garden suburb should not automatically be treated as the full Garden City
model. An orchard or allotment is a discussion component, not evidence of its
land-ownership or settlement system.
[TCPA history](https://www.tcpa.org.uk/about/our-history/),
[CIP history](https://www.cip-icu.ca/our-history/).

Civic improvement is another strand. Ottawa's successive improvement and
capital plans connected public space, waterfronts, infrastructure and metropolitan
image. The NCC traces the 1899 Improvement Commission, subsequent planning visions
and Gréber's 1950 railway-relocation proposals. This supports comparisons between
formal civic composition and wider systems of movement and land use; a dome or
symmetrical city hall alone cannot establish a City Beautiful plan.
[National Capital Commission](https://ncc-ccn.gc.ca/blog/big-ideas-that-built-canadas-capital).

Interwar architecture has a separate chronology. Bauhaus operated 1919–1933;
De Stijl formed around a magazine founded in 1917, and the Rietveld Schröder House
dates to 1924. The new workshop and studio are contemporary original teaching
designs referencing those movements. They are not postwar buildings merely
because Modernism later became important in Canadian cities.
[UNESCO Bauhaus history](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/1954/),
[Rietveld Schröder House](https://www.rietveldschroderhuis.nl/en).

### Postwar growth and its competing consequences

CMHC's 1954 plan book illustrates bungalow and split-level house forms. Toronto's
account of Don Mills describes a 1950s planned community with modernist houses,
winding streets and culs-de-sac. Its employment-area history records the
importance of automobile access and major roads. These are useful comparative
cases for housing production, road hierarchy, local facilities and everyday trips;
they are not a single description of every Canadian suburb.
[CMHC 1954](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.821302/publication.html),
[Don Mills housing](https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/history-art-culture/online-exhibits/web-exhibits/web-exhibits-community-neighbourhoods/progress-economy-heart-celebrating-100-years-of-north-york/progress-economy-heart-living-in-north-york/),
[Don Mills employment and roads](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/90be-City-Planning-SIPA-DonMills-Crossing-Development-History.pdf).

Postwar Modernist architecture includes several forms and institutional
settings. Manitoba's guide explains the delayed local uptake of earlier
international ideas. A slab apartment, clerestory hall, Brutalist civic office
and concrete parkade should therefore be compared through their specific
forms, uses and street relationships. Concrete is a material; Modernism is an
architectural movement; Modernist planning is a further urban-scale question.
[Manitoba Modern architecture guide](https://www.gov.mb.ca/chc/hrb/internal_reports/pdfs/modern_architecture_brief_guide.pdf).

Urban renewal also involved displacement and unequal treatment. Halifax's
historical planning account records Africville's destruction and residents'
displacement. Vancouver records demolition in Strathcona, opposition to
redevelopment/freeway proposals, and a 1968 rehabilitation agreement. These
histories belong alongside accounts of new housing and infrastructure rather
than being reduced to a visual preference between old and new buildings.
[Halifax historical account](https://cdn.halifax.ca/sites/default/files/documents/about-the-city/regional-community-planning/Centre_Plan_Package_A_Ministerial_Approved_Version_Nov_22_2019_ONLINE.pdf),
[Vancouver Strathcona history](https://vancouver.ca/news-calendar/strathcona.aspx).

### Community action, conservation and housing institutions

Community opposition and rehabilitation challenged clearance-led planning.
Toronto's records connect Jane Jacobs to opposition to the Spadina Expressway;
the City's transport history records the 1971 commitment limiting its extension
south of Eglinton. These cases help students ask how local knowledge and public
participation can change a plan.
[Toronto civic recognition](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/2006/minutes/council/cc060425.pdf),
[Spadina historical account](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2013/pw/bgrd/backgroundfile-61472.pdf).

Housing institutions deserve equal attention. Toronto identifies the planned
St Lawrence neighbourhood of mid-rise apartments and low-rise townhouses opening
east of George Street in 1977. Housing research describes its mix of uses,
incomes and public, non-profit, co-operative and market housing. Those
arrangements require documentary evidence: an attractive courtyard or a model
named Affordable is insufficient to establish rent, ownership or governance.
[City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2008/te/bgrd/backgroundfile-9479.pdf),
[University of Toronto housing research](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/mixed-income-housing-one-answer-out-control-home-prices).

## Recent approaches and the categories students will see

| Approach | Useful distinction for students |
| --- | --- |
| Vancouverism | A tower-and-podium/public-realm urban-design approach; the individual model is one component. |
| Missing-middle housing | House-scale multi-unit forms and clusters; historic examples and contemporary interpretations can share the category. |
| Compact infill | Development within built-up areas; includes forms beyond missing middle, such as detached infill or larger buildings. |
| Transit-oriented development | Land use, walking, cycling and useful transit work together; a station alone is insufficient. |
| New Urbanism | A planning movement involving connected neighbourhoods, mixed uses, housing choices and public space. |
| Complete neighbourhoods | Access to everyday services, homes and community facilities; related to, but not identical with, a particular architectural aesthetic. |
| Complete Streets, walking/cycling and shared streets | Different ways of allocating and connecting movement and social space; operating conditions matter. |
| Green infrastructure and landscape connections | Water, habitat, planting and public use as systems; visible greenery does not establish measured performance. |
| Heritage continuity and adaptive reuse | Separate the period of the inherited reference from the period and purpose of adaptation. |
| Public life and child-friendly places | Investigate who can use the space, for which activities, and through what routes. |

The City of Vancouver describes Vancouverism through slender towers, lower
edges, light and views, parks, walkability and sustainable movement. That makes the
two reviewed residential tower/podium examples useful components. An office
tower is not assigned Vancouverism merely because it is glazed.
[City of Vancouver](https://vancouver.ca/home-property-development/planning-zoning-development.aspx).

Recent planning references are dated. Vancouver's city-wide Official Development
Plan was adopted on 31 March 2026 and replaced Vancouver Plan 2022. Its complete-
neighbourhood directions sit alongside reconciliation, equity and resilience.
CMHC's present housing catalogue also connects current modest multi-unit
designs with the earlier national plan-book tradition. These are current cases
checked on 25 October 2026, not universal rules for Canadian municipalities.
[Vancouver ODP](https://vancouver.ca/home-property-development/vancouver-official-development-plan.aspx),
[plan replacement](https://vancouver.ca/home-property-development/zoning-land-use-document-library-amendments.aspx),
[CMHC catalogue](https://www.housingcatalogue.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/about).

## Examples of the resulting classification

### Housing tenure is separate from building form

Tenure describes the arrangement through which people occupy a home, such as
ownership, rental or co-operative occupancy. Public and non-profit describe
housing providers; affordability describes a relationship between housing cost
and household resources. These fields should be taught separately rather than
inferred from a facade. CMHC's glossary distinguishes the range of tenures and
community-housing providers; Statistics Canada also records dwelling structure,
condominium status and tenure as separate variables.
[CMHC glossary](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/nhs/guidepage-strategy/glossary?rdlink=1),
[Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=9810023901).

The following are possible classroom scenarios using existing forms, not claims
about the catalogue models' actual ownership or operation:

| Existing form | Possible tenure/provider scenario |
| --- | --- |
| Fourplex or duplex | Owner-occupied unit with other units rented; or a rental building |
| Courtyard cottages or townhouses | Co-operative occupancy; private rental; or individual ownership |
| Mid-rise apartment building | Private rental, non-profit rental or condominium ownership |
| Residential tower and podium | Rental or condominium housing |

A new housing-tenure lesson therefore need not require a new architectural
appearance. A student could assign a documented or proposed tenure and provider
to their project while reusing these forms. No catalogue tenure is automatically
assigned in this classification.

The seven uninstalled candidates expand comparison: Prairie School relates to
early 20th-century American domestic architecture; the Postmodern library to
late 20th-century architectural communication; Bauhaus and De Stijl to interwar
European movements; the Mughal-inspired pavilion to a South Asian historical
reference. Their Canadian connections are teaching comparisons, not claims that
these traditions originated in Canada.
[Prairie School](https://www.flwright.org/explore/prairie-style),
[Mughal court-city reference](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/255/).

The two housing additions connect architecture to housing delivery. The Victory
house illustrates modest wartime/postwar form, while the side-split separates
living and sleeping spaces through staggered levels. The federal local inventory
and CMHC's 1954 plan-book provide context; the generated designs are not exact
historic plans. Their records remain separate from the 196 current choices.
[Victory Housing](https://veterans.gc.ca/en/remembrance/memorials/canada/victory-housing),
[CMHC 1954](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.821302/publication.html).

## Classroom applications and remaining gaps

Three short exercises connect the catalogue to planning rather than memorising
style names:

1. Compare a historic duplex/main-street arrangement, a postwar bungalow/retail-
   strip arrangement and a contemporary fourplex arrangement. Discuss daily
   trips, land use, public/private edges and housing choices without assuming rents.
2. Rework a redevelopment proposal through conservation, adaptive reuse and new
   construction alternatives. Ask which residents, businesses and public uses
   each option retains or displaces, and what evidence the decision needs.
3. Design a transit-linked complete neighbourhood with streets, homes, services
   and parks. Explain why isolated transit, greenery or tower components cannot
   establish the performance of the whole plan.

The largest gaps are institutional and relational: Indigenous authority,
tenure/non-market housing, displacement, regional infrastructure and whole-
neighbourhood outcomes. These need sourced case studies or composed editable
scenes. More facade styles alone would not fill them. Source-only families are
research leads until their exact variants and model identities are reviewed.

## Evidence and reuse

The authored history layer preserves the existing locked style audit and model
inventory. Its own input lock binds the new framework, 196 exact interpretations
and seven candidate records. Altered inputs, new model revisions, held identities
and incomplete coverage are rejected or withheld. No model, seed, placement or
publication approval is changed by a research classification.

The companion CSVs provide 203 current/candidate rows and 521 family-coverage
rows (497 legacy families plus 24 current-only native families). Source facts and
access/status notes are recorded in
`tools/planning_history/reviewed-inputs/framework.json`; each classification
also carries its design evidence, rationale and source references. Previously
curated architectural sources are distinguished from references checked for
this research. The dates remain interpretive ranges suitable for introductory
discussion, not measured construction histories.

The local catalogue now supports combined use, style, era and planning filters,
plus concise historical context on expanded cards. Development mode includes
one local plaza review model that test/production mode excludes; this is an
existing availability gate, not a new publication approval. The classification
snapshot retains all 196 local records, while browsing offers only the choices
available in the running environment.
